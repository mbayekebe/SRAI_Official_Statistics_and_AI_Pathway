# OS-A01-A05 Video Narration Script

**Estimated duration:** approximately 20 minutes  
**Rule:** Speaker notes contain audience narration only.

## Slide 1: The National Information Ecosystem

Welcome to OS-A01, The National Information Ecosystem. This lesson examines the system in which official statistics are authorized, produced, exchanged, interpreted and used. We will work from the needs of public decision makers and citizens back through data flows, institutions, safeguards and evidence. The central question is practical: before an institution adopts artificial intelligence, does it understand the information system that AI would depend on? By the end of the lesson, you should be able to map that system, identify material gaps, distinguish evidence from assumptions and screen an AI opportunity without overstating readiness.

## Slide 2: Learning outcomes

The lesson has four outcomes. First, you will define a defensible ecosystem boundary and identify the actors inside it. Second, you will trace information from its source through transformations and handoffs to the decision it informs. Third, you will assess whether the available information has the required quality, authority, security and accountability. Finally, you will screen a proposed AI use case and decide whether the evidence supports proceeding, improving the underlying system first or stopping. These outcomes treat AI readiness as an institutional and statistical question, rather than a software procurement question.

## Slide 3: The information ecosystem

An information ecosystem includes more than datasets. It includes the people and institutions that produce or use information, the assets they maintain, the rules that authorize their actions, the processes and technology that move information, and the accountability arrangements that govern decisions. The word ecosystem matters because these elements interact. A technically sound dataset can still fail if no institution has authority to maintain it, if handoffs are undocumented or if users cannot interpret its limitations. A credible map therefore connects institutional roles, information assets, transformations, safeguards and decisions inside a clearly stated boundary.

## Slide 4: Three nested boundaries

Ecosystem mapping becomes manageable when we distinguish three nested boundaries. The broadest boundary is the national information ecosystem, which includes official and nonofficial information relevant to public decisions. Within it sits the national statistical system and the institutions responsible for official statistics. The narrowest boundary is a specific product or use case, such as agricultural production estimates or an early warning indicator. A readiness claim only has meaning when it names the boundary. An institution may have strong capability for one statistical product while the wider national system still has serious coordination, access or quality gaps.

## Slide 5: Roles and institutional authority

An institution can perform several roles, but the roles should not be confused. A producer creates or compiles information. A methodological authority defines standards, classifications and quality requirements. A custodian maintains records and controls access. A coordinator aligns institutions and resolves gaps or duplication. The same organization may produce a survey, maintain its archive and issue methodological guidance, yet each function carries a different accountability. Mapping roles at this level helps reveal where authority is missing, where mandates overlap and where an AI system could accidentally bypass a required institutional decision.

## Slide 6: Information assets

A useful asset inventory covers more than files stored in a database. Administrative records may capture transactions or service delivery. Surveys and censuses provide designed observations for defined populations. Geospatial and sensor data add location and environmental measurements. Reference information includes classifications, metadata and business rules that make other assets interpretable. For each asset, record the owner, purpose, population, geography, reference period, frequency, transformations, access conditions and known quality limitations. AI cannot repair an asset whose meaning, coverage or provenance remains unknown.

## Slide 7: Information flows and transformations

A flow map follows information from source to use. At each handoff, identify the sending and receiving actors, the format, timing, transformation, control and potential failure. Validation deserves special attention because a dataset can move successfully while its meaning changes or its quality deteriorates. The map should also show where records support each step, such as a transfer log, validation report, approval or revision note. When responsibility and evidence follow the data, teams can locate failures, correct them and explain what happened. This traceability becomes essential when an AI system adds another transformation to the chain.

## Slide 8: Quality and safeguards

Readiness requires both statistical quality and institutional safeguards. Statistical quality asks whether information is relevant, accurate, timely, coherent and accessible for its intended use. Safeguards ask whether the actors have authority, whether confidentiality and security controls are adequate, whether the process can be audited and whether a responsible person remains accountable for the decision. A technically accurate model can still be unacceptable if it uses data without authority or if no one can explain and challenge its output. The intended decision determines which quality dimensions and safeguards matter most.

## Slide 9: Evidence labels

Every material statement in the map should carry an evidence label. Evidenced means that a document, dataset, system record or validated observation supports the statement. Reported means that a knowledgeable person stated it, but the team has not independently confirmed it. Unknown means that the information is unavailable, conflicting or still under review. These labels prevent confidence from increasing merely because a statement appears repeatedly in meetings or reports. They also create a practical workplan. Reported claims require confirmation, and unknowns require investigation or an explicit decision to proceed with uncertainty.

## Slide 10: Fictional case: Republic of Kintaba

The practical work uses the fictional Republic of Kintaba. The case includes twelve actors, ten information assets, eight flows, eight evidence records, six risks and five candidate AI opportunities. Kintaba faces realistic problems: agricultural estimates come from several sources, actor responsibilities overlap, classifications differ and some handoffs depend on informal processes. Because the case is synthetic, learners can examine institutional weaknesses without exposing confidential country information. The task is to build a defensible explanation of the ecosystem and use that explanation to screen proposed AI applications.

## Slide 11: Exercise sequence

The exercise sequence moves from the institutional map to an AI decision. Exercise one identifies actors, roles, evidence and authority. Exercise two inventories the information assets and tests whether their ownership and quality are clear. Exercise three traces a critical flow from source to decision, including transformations and failure points. Exercise four screens a proposed AI opportunity using the evidence gathered in the earlier steps. At every stage, record what is evidenced, what remains reported and what is unknown. The quality of the final recommendation depends on this discipline.

## Slide 12: AI opportunity screening

AI opportunity screening begins with the decision or service that should improve, not with a preferred technology. Describe the current baseline and identify the data assets that would support the task. Then assess quality limitations and the authority required to use the data and act on the output. The final decision has three defensible forms. Proceed when the evidence and safeguards support a controlled pilot. Improve first when material gaps can be addressed. Stop when the purpose, authority, evidence or risk profile does not support further work. A stop decision can be a successful result of responsible screening.

## Slide 13: Common failure patterns

Several failure patterns weaken ecosystem diagnostics. An inventory without a defined purpose produces long lists that do not explain decisions. Teams may assume that data access or operational ownership provides legal authority, although those are separate questions. Composite readiness scores can hide a critical weakness if strong dimensions average out a missing safeguard. Finally, selecting an AI tool first creates pressure to reinterpret the evidence as justification. A credible diagnostic does the opposite. It makes uncertainty, disagreement and missing authority visible so that leaders can act on the real constraints.

## Slide 14: From diagnostic to action plan

The diagnostic should lead to a sequenced action plan. In the first ninety days, confirm mandates, ownership and the most important evidence gaps. Over the next three to six months, repair priority flows, metadata and quality controls. Once those foundations are credible, run controlled pilots with explicit statistical assurance and safeguards. Continue reviewing outcomes, risks and public value after deployment. Each action needs a named owner, an evidence requirement, a deadline and a decision point. This turns the ecosystem map into a management instrument rather than a one-time workshop output.

## Slide 15: Assessment evidence

Assessment focuses on the quality of reasoning and evidence. A strong submission states and justifies its boundary, identifies actors and authority with appropriate evidence, traces a critical information flow and reaches a realistic AI screening decision. It also makes uncertainty and safeguards explicit. The assessment does not reward optimism about AI or the volume of material collected. It rewards a coherent argument that another reviewer could inspect, challenge and reproduce. This standard supports accountable decisions in official statistics, where the consequences of poor information can extend far beyond the immediate project.

## Slide 16: One method across multiple public decisions

The ecosystem method applies across public sectors. Agriculture, education, health and habitat rely on different actors, assets and decisions, yet each domain needs a clear boundary, traceable evidence, statistical quality and accountable authority. This section moves from the general method to the Kintaba evidence package. We will examine the risk register and the candidate AI opportunities to see how a diagnostic changes the decision. The objective is not to rank technologies. It is to identify which proposal, if any, has enough institutional and statistical support for a controlled next step.

## Slide 17: Kintaba evidence package

The Kintaba laboratory uses seven linked datasets. The evidence package describes twelve actors, ten information assets, eight flows, five needs, eight evidence records, six material risks and five candidate AI opportunities. These files connect institutional claims to reproducible analytical outputs. Actor and asset identifiers support referential integrity. Risk and opportunity records preserve the reasoning behind each recommendation. This structure allows a reviewer to trace a conclusion back to its source rather than relying on a presentation summary alone.

## Slide 18: Material risk exposure

The Kintaba risk register multiplies likelihood by impact to show relative exposure. Inconsistent concepts and definitions has the highest score at twenty-five. Coverage bias, missing identifiers and overgeneralizing pilot accuracy each score twenty. Weak continuity scores sixteen, while confidentiality or unlawful reuse scores fifteen. These values do not replace judgment, but they show why technical integration cannot begin with data merging alone. The leading mitigation is to agree classifications, metadata and revision policy before integration. Without that foundation, additional automation can make inconsistencies spread faster.

## Slide 19: Five candidate AI opportunities

The five AI opportunities share a clear pattern. Data readiness ranges from two to three on a five-point scale, while methodological risk ranges from four to five. Readiness therefore remains below risk for every proposal. The crop-condition nowcast and crop-stress detection have enough value to justify only controlled pilots. Estimate reconciliation requires metadata and provenance work first. Holding-frame prioritization remains a research prototype. Irrigation coverage gap detection should not be automated until the source data improve. The portfolio view prevents a promising title from obscuring weak foundations.

## Slide 20: Disposition by opportunity

Each opportunity receives a disposition that reflects the evidence. Controlled pilot only means the team may test a bounded use case with explicit validation and stopping rules. Metadata and provenance first means the institution should repair traceability before modeling. Research prototype separates exploration from operational claims. Improve source data before automation means that the current information cannot support an accountable automated decision. These labels create different pathways, rather than forcing every proposal into a single approval pipeline.

## Slide 21: Controlled pilot boundary

A controlled pilot needs a precise boundary. Name one decision or service, define the population and exclusions, specify the evidence and comparison, and identify the approver and operator. The pilot also needs exit conditions that state when to stop, revise or consider scaling. This structure prevents a successful demonstration from becoming an informal authorization for general deployment. A pilot tests a bounded claim under stated conditions. Broader use requires new evidence and a separate decision.

## Slide 22: Statistical assurance record

Statistical assurance turns a pilot result into inspectable evidence. Record the baseline process and its error profile. Choose validity metrics that match the intended use and examine performance across relevant subgroups or domains. Define drift monitoring and triggers. Preserve provenance, code, versions and parameters so another analyst can reproduce the result. Require independent challenge and approval before release. The release record should state scope, limitations and revision arrangements. This evidence supports accountability after the original project team has moved on.

## Slide 23: Institutional action priorities

The institutional action plan starts with mandates, classifications and evidence gaps. The next priority is to repair critical flows, identifiers and metadata. Only then should teams run bounded pilots with statistical assurance. Risk, public value and accountability require continuing review throughout the lifecycle. This sequence strengthens the information ecosystem even when an AI proposal does not proceed. That is an important measure of value: the diagnostic improves the institution’s ability to produce and use trustworthy official statistics.

## Slide 24: Executive authorization

A defensible AI decision begins with trustworthy official statistics. Proceed when the evidence supports a bounded pilot with clear assurance and accountability. Improve first when material gaps can be repaired before testing. Stop when the purpose, authority, evidence or safeguards remain insufficient. The Kintaba laboratory now asks you to apply these distinctions and preserve the evidence record. The goal is not to approve the largest number of AI projects. The goal is to make decisions that institutions can explain, review and defend.
